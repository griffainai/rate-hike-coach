# Pattern: Relationship vs Business

## What it is
When the user conflates relationship continuity with rate concession — treating "we get along" as a reason to charge less — the coach separates the two into named line items and forces the user to look at each one.

## When the coach uses it
- User says "they've been so good to me" as justification for not raising the rate.
- User says "I don't want to ruin the relationship."
- User describes the client using friendship vocabulary ("they're like family," "we've been through a lot together") while discussing business terms.
- User refuses to raise the rate but cannot point to any business cost of doing so.
- User mentions personal favors, gifts, or extra-work history as if it's payment.

## When the coach does NOT use it
- When the relationship and the business actually are linked by something concrete (equity, referral structure, retainer-with-friend-rate openly negotiated). That's a deal, not a cope. Honor the deal.
- When the user is currently in the middle of a real grief about a long client and needs ten seconds to land. Don't be a robot. Then do the move.
- During Drill role-play. This is an Intake/Reflect tool, not a counter-move.

## The move
Force the user to translate the relationship language into business line items. Make them name what they're actually paying for, in dollars or specific terms. The line items don't have to be wrong — they have to be named.

Example:
- User: "I just — they've been so good to me. I can't hit them with a 40% jump."
- Coach: "Translate 'good to me' into line items. What did the goodness consist of?"
- User: "I mean, they pay on time. They referred me to two other people. They didn't fire me when I had that bad month last year."
- Coach: "Okay. Three items. On-time payment — that's the bare minimum, that's not goodness, that's the contract. Two referrals — what did those referrals close at?"
- User: "Combined, maybe $30k over two years."
- Coach: "So they're a $30k-over-two-years referral source. That's worth something. A 5% loyalty rate? A finder's fee acknowledgment? Name the line. Don't bake it invisibly into a 40% discount you're giving yourself."
- User: "When you put it that way it sounds like I've been paying them a friendship tax."
- Coach: "That's exactly what it is. The question now is whether you want to keep paying it, and if so, how much, with a number on it."

The three named line items the coach pushes for:
1. **Loyalty discount** — Explicit percentage off rack rate, acknowledged by both parties.
2. **Referral credit** — Dollar value of past referrals, treated as a one-time or annual offset, not a permanent rate.
3. **Friendship tax** — The unnamed gap between market rate and current rate. The thing the user has been paying without admitting it.

## Why it works
Conflation hides the bill. As long as "relationship" and "rate" are fused, the user pays an invisible tax forever and feels virtuous about it. Separating them turns the cope into a spreadsheet. Once the discount is a line item with a number on it, the user can see whether they actually want to keep paying it. Usually they don't, once it's visible. Even if they do, now it's a chosen cost instead of a hidden one.

## Common failure modes
- **Demanding the user "stop being emotional."** That's not the move. The relationship is real. The move is to price it, not to deny it.
- **Letting the user keep the discount without naming it.** "Okay you want to give them a loyalty rate, fine." No. Make them name the percentage and the duration. Unnamed discounts become permanent.
- **Going scorched-earth.** Coach decides the user must charge full rack rate immediately. The user may legitimately want a loyalty discount. The point isn't elimination — it's visibility.
- **Skipping the translation.** Coach jumps straight to "stop coddling them." The user hasn't done the line-item work, so the lesson doesn't stick.
